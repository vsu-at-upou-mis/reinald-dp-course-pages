Topic 6 — Software Development Ethics

A guided reading on the ethical responsibilities that run through the software lifecycle — eight dimensions of responsible development, professional codes of ethics, the four ethical frameworks, and what to do when responsibilities conflict.

Faculty-in-Charge: Dr. Reinald Adrian Pugoy — First Semester, AY 2026–2027
01 · Introduction

Software Development as an Ethical Practice

Software development involves decisions that affect people's privacy, security, access to services, opportunities, and safety. Ethical responsibility therefore extends beyond creating software that simply works.

It requires considering how software is designed, developed, deployed, maintained, and used, including its consequences for individuals, organizations, and society.

Software development involves continuous choices about system requirements, data collection, user needs, acceptable risks, and deployment readiness. Although these decisions may appear technical, they often have ethical consequences.

Consider a developer who discovers a serious security vulnerability shortly before a scheduled launch. Fixing it would delay deployment, while ignoring it would meet the deadline but potentially expose users to harm.

Two different questions

The technical question
  • Can the system be deployed?
The ethical question
  • Should it be deployed?

Why This Matters to Information Systems Students and Professionals

Information systems professionals may work as developers, analysts, project managers, system administrators, consultants, or technology leaders. Even those who do not write code directly make decisions that influence how information systems are designed, deployed, and maintained.

These decisions may involve:

  • defining requirements;
  • evaluating risks;
  • protecting user information;
  • assessing software quality; or
  • approving system deployment.

Each carries potential ethical consequences.

Not limited to programmers — Software development ethics concerns everyone who makes or influences decisions throughout the information system lifecycle.

02 · Eight Dimensions of Responsible Development

Quality, Reliability, and Professional Responsibility

Responsible software development encompasses eight interconnected dimensions, each addressing a particular aspect of professional responsibility throughout the software lifecycle.

Developers have an ethical responsibility to produce software with quality, reliability, and safety appropriate to its purpose.

Software need not be perfect, but it should meet reasonable professional standards considering its intended use and the consequences of failure.

A similar defect in…May cause…
A recreational applicationInconvenience
A financial, medical, or government systemSubstantial harm

Professional responsibility also requires developers to work within their competence, follow appropriate development practices, and recognize significant technical limitations.

This dimension is about setting the right bar. How teams find out whether the software actually meets that bar is covered later under Testing, Quality Assurance, and Deployment.

Secure Software Development

Security should be considered throughout the software development lifecycle, rather than added only after implementation.

Poor development practices can introduce vulnerabilities through insecure authentication, improper access controls, exposed credentials, outdated dependencies, or insecure configurations. These weaknesses may lead to data breaches, fraud, service disruption, or other harm.

Developers should therefore
  • Follow secure coding practices
  • Protect credentials and sensitive information
  • Implement appropriate authentication and access controls
  • Maintain and update dependencies
  • Conduct security testing
  • Address known vulnerabilities
Known risks matter — Not every vulnerability can be prevented. However, knowingly leaving significant security risks unresolved raises ethical concerns. This connects directly with the cybersecurity responsibilities discussed in Topic 4.
04 · Eight Dimensions of Responsible Development

Privacy by Design

Developers influence privacy through decisions about what information systems collect, store, process, share, and retain.

Consider — Should an application collect a user's location if its functionality does not require it?

The ability to collect information does not automatically justify doing so. Unnecessary collection increases the potential for misuse, unauthorized access, or unintended secondary use.

Privacy by design means incorporating privacy considerations from the beginning of development. This includes:

  • collecting only necessary information;
  • clearly communicating data collection purposes;
  • limiting access to personal information;
  • establishing appropriate retention periods;
  • protecting sensitive data; and
  • providing meaningful user control.

As discussed in Topic 3, principles such as consent, data minimization, purpose limitation, and data stewardship must be translated into technical and design decisions.

05 · Eight Dimensions of Responsible Development

User-Centered, Inclusive, and Ethical Design

Software should be designed with consideration for the people who will use it, including their abilities, circumstances, and limitations.

A system may function correctly but remain difficult to use for people with disabilities, limited connectivity, older devices, or lower levels of digital literacy.

Ethical design therefore considers:

Accessibility Usability Device & bandwidth limits Language Cultural context User autonomy

Inclusive design does not require accommodating every possible user. It requires identifying relevant users and avoiding unnecessary barriers.

Dark Patterns and Manipulative Design

Ethical interface design also requires respecting users' ability to make informed choices.

Dark patterns (also called deceptive design patterns) — interface practices that manipulate, mislead, or obstruct users.
PatternExample
ObstructionMaking cancellation unnecessarily difficult
SneakingConcealing charges or important information
Interface interferenceMaking one choice prominent while obscuring alternatives
Forced actionRequiring unnecessary information or actions to access a service
False urgencyUsing misleading countdowns or availability claims
Working as intended. Dark patterns illustrate that software can function exactly as intended while still being ethically problematic.

The central question

Does the design support informed user choices, or manipulate users toward particular decisions?

06 · Eight Dimensions of Responsible Development

Testing, Quality Assurance, and Deployment

Section 02 set the standard software should meet. This dimension asks the next question: has that standard actually been met, and is the system ready to go live?

Quality vs. testing

Software quality
  • Establishes the expected standard
Testing & QA
  • Provide evidence of whether that standard has been met

Testing and quality assurance help determine whether software meets its requirements and is ready for deployment. They are important forms of technical verification and risk management.

Ethical concerns arise when developers discover defects, conduct insufficient testing, or face pressure to release software despite unresolved risks.

Scenario — A development team discovers that a system occasionally produces incorrect user records. Management insists on proceeding with deployment to meet the deadline.

The ethical issue is not simply the existence of defects, but their:

  1. Severity
  2. Likelihood
  3. Consequences
  4. Potential impact on users

Practices such as quality assurance, user acceptance testing, staged deployment, monitoring, and rollback procedures help organizations make informed deployment decisions.

07 · Eight Dimensions of Responsible Development

Maintenance and Software Sustainability

Software development does not end at deployment. Systems require ongoing updates, security patches, monitoring, documentation, and eventual replacement or retirement.

Technical debt — the result of short-term solutions that increase future maintenance costs or complexity.

Technical debt is not inherently unethical. It becomes ethically significant when unresolved issues create substantial risks that organizations knowingly ignore.

Software sustainability therefore involves:

Maintaining reliability+ Managing technical debt+ Updating unsupported components+ Communicating maintenance risks+ Planning replacement or retirement
08 · Eight Dimensions of Responsible Development

Code Reuse and Intellectual Property

Modern software development relies heavily on open-source software, libraries, frameworks, APIs, code repositories, and AI-generated code. Reusing code improves efficiency but introduces legal, technical, and ethical responsibilities.

Publicly accessible code is not necessarily free from restrictions. Software licenses may impose conditions on modification, distribution, attribution, and reuse.

Developers should consider
  • Software licenses and their conditions
  • Proper attribution and license compatibility
  • Ownership of proprietary code
  • Security and reliability of third-party dependencies
  • Whether reused or generated code is appropriate for the project

For example, code published in a public repository may still have licensing restrictions. Similarly, proprietary code developed for an employer or client should not be reused without appropriate authorization.

Code reuse also creates technical responsibilities. External libraries may contain vulnerabilities, become unsupported, or behave unexpectedly.

AI-generated code introduces additional concerns regarding correctness, security, originality, and potential intellectual property issues. Developers should review and understand generated code rather than assuming it is automatically suitable for use.

The developer remains responsible.

Developers remain responsible for code incorporated into their systems, regardless of its source.

09 · Eight Dimensions of Responsible Development

Transparency and Documentation

Software development depends on accurate communication among developers, administrators, managers, clients, and users. Ethical professional practice requires honesty about system capabilities, limitations, progress, and risks.

Developers should not knowingly misrepresent:

  • system capabilities;
  • testing results;
  • security status;
  • project progress;
  • known defects; or
  • deployment risks.

Documentation should record important requirements, design decisions, testing results, changes, and known limitations.

Why documentation matters — Good documentation supports maintenance, continuity, informed decision-making, and accountability, particularly when systems are maintained by people who were not involved in their original development.

11 · Professional Standards and Judgment

Professional Codes of Ethics

Computing professionals are guided not only by personal judgment but also by professional codes of ethics that establish responsibilities toward users, employers, clients, colleagues, and society.

Two relevant codes are the ACM Code of Ethics and Professional Conduct and the ACM/IEEE-CS Software Engineering Code of Ethics and Professional Practice.

ACM Code of Ethics and Professional Conduct

The ACM Code emphasizes responsibilities to contribute to human well-being, avoid harm, act honestly, respect privacy, maintain professional competence, produce high-quality work, evaluate risks, and develop secure systems.

These principles reinforce that professional responsibility extends beyond technical correctness to include the consequences of software for people and society.

Software Engineering Code of Ethics and Professional Practice

The ACM/IEEE-CS Software Engineering Code identifies eight principles:

  1. Public — Act consistently with the public interest.
  2. Client and Employer — Serve legitimate client and employer interests while respecting the public interest.
  3. Product — Ensure that software meets appropriate professional standards.
  4. Judgment — Maintain integrity and independence in professional judgment.
  5. Management — Promote ethical software development and maintenance.
  6. Profession — Uphold the integrity and reputation of the profession.
  7. Colleagues — Treat colleagues fairly and support their professional development.
  8. Self — Maintain competence and pursue ethical professional practice.

These principles recognize that developers have responsibilities to multiple stakeholders. Meeting deadlines or following management instructions does not automatically justify decisions that expose users or the public to unreasonable risks.

Beyond the person who pays

Professional responsibility extends beyond satisfying the person or organization that requested or paid for the software.

Applying the Four Ethical Frameworks

Consider the following scenario:

Case: The banking app update — A development team is preparing to release an update to a mobile banking application. During testing, they discover that completed fund transfers are sometimes not immediately reflected in transaction histories. The transfers are processed correctly, but users may temporarily see outdated information. Management wants to release the update on schedule and fix the issue later.

How might the four ethical frameworks help evaluate this decision?

FrameworkApplied to the caseKey question
Consequentialism Releasing may provide new features sooner, but inaccurate transaction histories could cause confusion, duplicate transfers, financial mistakes, or loss of trust. What is the likelihood and severity of the potential benefits and harms?
Deontology Developers have duties to provide accurate information, communicate known defects, and protect users from preventable risks. Would releasing violate these duties, regardless of whether serious harm actually occurs?
Virtue Ethics A responsible developer shows honesty, competence, prudence, and courage by disclosing the defect, evaluating its significance, and raising concerns despite deadline pressure. What would a conscientious professional do?
Justice and Fairness The company benefits from meeting its schedule, while users bear the consequences of misleading information — and some users are more vulnerable to financial disruption than others. Is the distribution of benefits, risks, and burdens fair?

These frameworks do not automatically produce the same answer. Instead, they highlight different ethical considerations that should inform professional judgment.

Quick check

8. A team member asks: "Is it right that the company gets the benefit of an on-time release while customers — some of whom live paycheck to paycheck — carry the risk of making duplicate transfers?" Which framework is she using?

13 · Professional Standards and Judgment

When Responsibilities Conflict

Software development often involves competing responsibilities. These tensions may not have solutions that satisfy every stakeholder.

On one side…On the other…
Meeting deadlinesConducting adequate testing
Satisfying clientsProtecting users
Reducing costsImproving accessibility
Collecting useful dataProtecting privacy
Following management instructionsExercising professional judgment
Legal is not the same as ethical. Legal compliance is important, but it does not resolve every ethical question. Software may comply with applicable laws while still collecting unnecessary information, manipulating users, excluding certain groups, or creating avoidable risks.

Ethical judgment therefore requires identifying affected stakeholders, evaluating consequences, considering professional duties, and communicating significant concerns.

More than systems that function.

Ultimately, responsible software development requires more than creating systems that function. It requires sound professional judgment throughout the software lifecycle.
