IS 295 Manuscript Structure and Guidelines

A chapter-by-chapter guide to documenting the development and evaluation of an information system — from the problem and its context, through design, development, testing, and evaluation, to conclusions and recommendations.

Prepared by: Dr. Reinald Adrian Pugoy — First Semester, AY 2026–2027
01 · Overview

Purpose of the Manuscript

The IS 295 manuscript documents the development and evaluation of an information system or information systems solution in response to an identified real-world problem, need, or opportunity.

The manuscript should demonstrate the student's ability to:

  1. Analyze a problem.
  2. Ground the solution in relevant literature and frameworks.
  3. Systematically design and develop an appropriate information system.
  4. Evaluate the resulting system using suitable technical and user-centered methods.

IS 295a: Proposal Stage

IS 295a covers Chapters I to IV. At this stage, the manuscript presents the problem to be addressed, establishes the academic and practical basis for the study, describes the intended system and its design, and specifies how the system will be developed, tested, and evaluated.

Because IS 295a represents the proposal stage, future-oriented language may be used where activities have not yet been completed. However, the proposal should already present a sufficiently developed and feasible plan rather than a preliminary idea. The student should be able to demonstrate that:

  • the problem has been adequately investigated;
  • the proposed solution is justified; and
  • the development and evaluation methods are appropriate and achievable.

IS 295b: Completed Study

IS 295b covers the complete manuscript, Chapters I to VI, including the developed system, results of technical validation and user evaluation, conclusions, and recommendations.

The IS 295b manuscript should read as a completed study rather than a proposal. Chapters I to IV should therefore be updated to reflect what was undertaken according to the approved IS 295a plan. Future-oriented statements from the IS 295a proposal should be replaced with appropriate descriptions of the actual system, procedures, participants, technologies, development activities, testing methods, and evaluation procedures.

IS 295a (Proposal)IS 295b (Completed Study)
"The study will develop...""The study developed..."
"The proposed system will provide...""The system provides..." or "The developed system provides..."
"The researchers will conduct user testing...""User testing was conducted..."
"The system will be evaluated using...""The system was evaluated using..."

The approved IS 295a proposal is your blueprint.

IS 295b should carry out the objectives, scope, design, and methodology approved in IS 295a as closely as possible. Revising Chapters I–IV mainly means updating them from future to past or present tense and confirming what was done according to plan. It does not mean redesigning the study to match whatever was built.

Any substantive change should be justified and approved by your adviser.

03 · The Six Chapters

Chapter I. Introduction

This chapter establishes the foundation of the study by presenting the problem context, rationale, objectives, significance, and boundaries of the study.

A. Background of the Study

Provides the context and justification for the study or project. This section should explain:

  • the real-world problem, unmet need, or operational challenge being addressed;
  • the broader domain or environment in which the problem exists;
  • existing systems, practices, or current approaches;
  • limitations, inefficiencies, or gaps in current approaches;
  • why the problem is important; and
  • why an information systems intervention is appropriate.

The discussion should progressively move from the broader context toward the specific problem addressed by the study.

Broader domain→ Current approaches→ Gaps & limitations→ Specific problem
Guiding question — What problem context gave rise to this study, and why does it matter?

B. Statement of the Problem

Clearly defines the specific problem or problems the study seeks to address. This section should:

  • translate the broader background into focused problem statements;
  • identify operational, technical, informational, organizational, or user-centered issues;
  • clarify what is currently lacking, ineffective, or inefficient; and
  • logically establish the need for the system or intervention developed in the study.

This may be presented as a general problem statement followed by specific research or project questions, where appropriate.

Guiding question — What exactly is the problem this study intends to solve?

C. Objectives of the Study

States what the study aims to accomplish.

Two levels of objectives

General Objective
  • States the overall purpose of the study.
Specific Objectives
  • Break down the general objective into concrete and assessable objectives.

Depending on the nature of the study, objectives may involve:

Analyzing processes & needs→ Specifying requirements→ Designing→ Developing & implementing→ Testing & validating→ Evaluating outcomes

Evaluation objectives may address usability, acceptance, effectiveness, performance, or other relevant outcomes. The objectives should align with the succeeding methodology, results, and conclusions.

Each specific objective must be assessable. Chapter IV-C states how its achievement will be demonstrated.

Guiding question — What does the study aim to achieve?

D. Significance of the Study

Explains who may benefit from the study and how. Potential beneficiaries may include:

  • end users;
  • organizations or institutions;
  • administrators and decision-makers;
  • operational personnel;
  • researchers;
  • future developers; and
  • broader communities or sectors.
Remember — Explain the specific value or contribution of the study to each relevant stakeholder rather than merely listing potential beneficiaries.
Guiding question — Who benefits from this study, and in what ways?

E. Scope and Limitations

Defines the boundaries of the study.

Scope clarifies, where applicable…Limitations identify constraints such as…
Target usersExcluded features or processes
Processes or workflows coveredDeployment constraints
Included modules and functionalitiesUnavailable integrations
Implementation or deployment environmentLimited participants or datasets
Datasets or information coveredTechnological constraints
Extent of testing and evaluationTime or resource constraints
Prototype-only or limited deployment, where applicable
Limitations are not excuses. Limitations should describe genuine boundaries or constraints of the work rather than serve as justification for avoidable shortcomings.
Guiding question — What does this study cover, and what falls outside its boundaries?
04 · The Six Chapters

Chapter II. Review of Related Works

This chapter situates the study within existing knowledge, prior studies, technologies, and comparable systems.

It should establish what is already known or available, identify relevant gaps, and demonstrate how the study is positioned in relation to existing work.

Analyze and synthesize — don't just summarize. The chapter should be analytical and synthetic rather than merely descriptive. Students should not simply summarize one study or system after another.

A. Related Studies and Existing Systems

Presents and synthesizes relevant academic studies, existing systems, technologies, and practical solutions related to the problem domain. Depending on the study, this may include:

  • prior research studies;
  • relevant theories and concepts;
  • existing platforms, applications, or information systems;
  • technological approaches;
  • methods used in comparable projects; and
  • relevant practical implementations.

The review should prioritize works that directly inform the problem, system design, development approach, or evaluation.

B. Comparative Analysis

Compares relevant existing systems, alternatives, or approaches. The comparison may examine:

Features· Capabilities· Target users· Strengths & weaknesses· Limitations· Technologies used· Relevance to the study

Where appropriate, a comparative matrix may be used, but it should be accompanied by analytical discussion.

C. Identified Gaps and Opportunities

Synthesizes the review and identifies unmet needs, unresolved limitations, or opportunities that provide the basis for the study. This section should clearly explain:

  1. What existing studies or solutions already address.
  2. What existing solutions fail to address adequately.
  3. What limitations or needs remain unresolved.
  4. What opportunity exists for an improved or alternative information systems solution.
Remember — The identified gap should logically emerge from the preceding review rather than simply assert that no similar system exists.

D. Conceptual Positioning of the System

Explains how the system developed in the study responds to the identified gaps and how it is positioned relative to existing alternatives. This section should clarify:

  • the intended contribution of the system;
  • the innovation, improvement, adaptation, or integration it introduces;
  • how it differs meaningfully from relevant existing solutions; and
  • why the study is warranted.

Proposed vs. developed

IS 295a
  • This section may discuss the proposed system.
IS 295b
  • Revise it to describe the system developed in the study.
05 · The Six Chapters

Chapter III. System Design and Development Methodology

This chapter explains how the system was conceptualized, designed, and developed.

For IS 295a, the chapter describes the planned design and development methodology. For IS 295b, it should document how this approved plan was carried out. Any substantive deviation should be justified and approved by your adviser.

Writing guidance. Present the methodology, design logic, and technical approach at an appropriate academic level of abstraction. Avoid turning this chapter into software engineering documentation, a developer manual, or a code-level implementation walkthrough. Excessively detailed technical artifacts should be placed in the Appendix when necessary.

A. Requirements Analysis

Explains how stakeholder needs and system requirements were identified and translated into system specifications. Include, where applicable:

  • stakeholder analysis;
  • needs assessment;
  • interviews, consultations, surveys, or observations;
  • current process or workflow analysis;
  • functional requirements;
  • non-functional requirements; and
  • use case identification.
Traceability — The requirements presented should be traceable to the problem and objectives established in Chapter I.

B. Theoretical and Conceptual Framework

Presents the frameworks that guide the design, development, or evaluation of the system. Include, where applicable:

  • theoretical framework or frameworks;
  • conceptual framework;
  • rationale for framework selection; and
  • explanation of how the framework informs the study, system design, or evaluation.
Remember — Frameworks should be meaningfully integrated into the study rather than included solely as a formal requirement.

C. System Development Approach

Explains the development methodology or approach adopted in creating the system. Examples may include Agile, Scrum, Waterfall, Rapid Application Development (RAD), iterative development, or another justified development approach.

Depending on the selected methodology, relevant phases may include:

Planning→ Analysis→ Design→ Development→ Testing during development→ Deployment→ Refinement / iteration
Apply, don't define — Explain how the selected approach was applied to the study, rather than merely providing a textbook definition of the methodology.

D. System Architecture and Technology Stack

Describes the technical architecture and implementation environment of the system. Include, where relevant:

ComponentWhat to describe
ArchitectureOverall system architecture
FrontendFrontend technologies
BackendBackend technologies
DataDatabases
IntegrationAPIs and integrations
InfrastructureInfrastructure or hosting environment
DecisionsMajor technical design decisions

The discussion should explain important technological choices and their relationship to system requirements rather than provide an exhaustive inventory of tools or implementation details.

E. System Design and Functional Modules

Presents the planned or implemented system design and its major components. Include, where applicable:

  • module overview;
  • process flows;
  • use case diagrams;
  • data flow diagrams;
  • entity relationship diagrams;
  • interface or interaction designs; and
  • major system features and modules.

The discussion should demonstrate how the system design responds to the identified requirements.

Mockups in IS 295a, screenshots in Chapter V

Chapter III · IS 295a
  • Interface mockups, wireframes, or prototypes may be included to communicate the intended design.
Chapter III · IS 295b
  • Move the mockups to the Appendices. Keep a mockup in Chapter III only when it is needed to explain a design decision.
Chapter V · IS 295b
  • Screenshots of the completed system and its implemented features are presented here.
Chapter III-E is not a copy of your SRS. The SRS is a separate document that states what the system must do. Chapter III-E explains how the design meets those requirements and why it was designed that way. Chapter III-A summarizes the key requirements, and Chapter III-E refers to them by their SRS IDs (e.g., FR-03 to FR-07) instead of reproducing them.

Write Chapter III-E by:

  1. Organizing by module, not by requirement. For each major module, explain its purpose, its key design decisions, and the requirements it satisfies.
  2. Showing traceability compactly. Use a single table that maps modules to SRS requirement IDs.
  3. Explaining design rationale. Discuss the alternatives considered, the trade-offs, and how the framework in Chapter III-B shaped the design.
  4. Interpreting every diagram. Follow each figure with a discussion of what it shows and how it addresses the requirements.
Red flags — "The system shall…" statements; requirement tables or text copied from the SRS; diagrams with no accompanying discussion; paragraphs that describe what the system does but never how or why; and code-level artifacts such as CRUD walkthroughs, exhaustive database documentation, complete API specifications, or source code listings, which belong in the Appendices.

F. Implementation Plan

Presents a realistic plan for developing the system in IS 295b. Describe the phases of development and the sequence of activities, consistent with the development approach in Chapter III-C. Include, where applicable:

  • the major phases and activities;
  • their sequence and dependencies;
  • the duration of each activity;
  • key milestones and deliverables, such as the completion of each development iteration, testing, user evaluation, and the final manuscript; and
  • scheduled adviser consultations.
Keep it feasible — The plan should be achievable within the IS 295b term. Allot adequate time for testing, user evaluation, and manuscript writing rather than devoting the entire term to development.

Present the schedule as a Gantt chart. Table 3.1 is a worked example for a hypothetical project: an online appointment system for a university health clinic. It shows how activities are sequenced and tied to the objectives, not content to reuse. Build your own plan from your own modules, tests, and evaluation activities.

Chapter IV. System Testing and Evaluation Methodology

This chapter explains how the system was or will be tested, validated, and evaluated.

For IS 295a, this chapter specifies the planned testing and evaluation procedures. For IS 295b, it should document how these approved procedures were carried out. Any substantive deviation should be justified and approved by your adviser.

Writing guidance. Focus on the testing strategy, validation procedures, and evaluation methodology. Avoid overwhelming the chapter with raw logs, repetitive screenshots, exhaustive bug reports, or full documentary evidence better suited for the Appendices.

A. Testing and Technical Validation

Describes how the technical correctness, reliability, and performance of the system will be or were verified. Depending on the system, this may include:

  • unit testing;
  • integration testing;
  • system testing;
  • functional testing;
  • user acceptance testing;
  • performance testing;
  • security testing, where relevant;
  • bug or issue tracking;
  • issue resolution and retesting; and
  • technical acceptance criteria.
Fit the strategy to the system — Not every project is required to use every type of testing listed above. The testing strategy should be appropriate to the nature, scale, objectives, and risks of the system.

The main manuscript should focus on the testing methodology and validation approach. Detailed test cases, test scripts, bug logs, and related evidence may be placed in the Appendix.

B. User Evaluation Procedures

Describes how the system will be or was evaluated by intended users or relevant stakeholders. Include, where applicable:

  • participant selection;
  • stakeholder or user groups;
  • evaluation environment;
  • evaluation scenarios and assigned tasks;
  • data collection procedures;
  • survey instruments or questionnaires;
  • Likert-scale criteria;
  • rubric-based assessment;
  • qualitative feedback collection; and
  • evaluation dimensions derived from the selected theoretical or conceptual framework.
Purpose over complexity — The evaluation method should be appropriate to the study objectives. Avoid adding statistical procedures simply for complexity. Analytical methods should be used only when they meaningfully answer the study's questions or objectives.

Detailed instruments, questionnaires, consent forms, task sheets, and similar materials may be placed in the Appendix.

C. Evaluation of Objectives

Show how you will demonstrate that each specific objective in Chapter I-C has been achieved. For each objective, state the expected outcome and the test or evaluation that will provide the evidence, including the criterion for success.

Chapter V. Results and Discussion

This chapter presents what was actually developed, what was found through testing and evaluation, and what those findings mean.

Chapter V forms part of IS 295b and is not required as a completed-results chapter during the IS 295a proposal stage.

Writing guidance. Present the completed system and synthesized findings rather than raw documentation or data dumps. The chapter should demonstrate the major outputs of the study, report the evidence obtained through testing and evaluation, and interpret the findings in relation to the objectives, requirements, frameworks, and relevant existing work.

A. Developed System and Major Features

Presents the completed system and demonstrates its major implemented features. Include, where appropriate:

  • overview of the completed system;
  • major modules and functionalities;
  • representative screenshots of important interfaces and features;
  • brief explanation of what each major feature does;
  • important workflows or user interactions; and
  • relationship of implemented features to identified requirements and study objectives.

Screenshots should be selective and purposeful. They should demonstrate significant features or support the discussion rather than serve as a complete visual tour of the application.

Each screenshot should be presented as a properly numbered and captioned figure and discussed in the text. For example:

Figure 5.1. Administrator Dashboard — The accompanying discussion should explain what the interface demonstrates, its function within the system, and its relevance to the requirements or objectives.
Not a complete visual tour. The manuscript should not include screenshots of every page, button, CRUD operation, form, or minor interface variation. Comprehensive interface documentation may instead be placed in the Appendix.

B. Technical Testing and Validation Results

Presents the results of the technical testing procedures described in Chapter IV. Include, where applicable:

  • functional testing results;
  • integration testing results;
  • system testing results;
  • performance findings;
  • security testing findings;
  • user acceptance testing results;
  • identified issues and their resolution; and
  • overall technical validation against established acceptance criteria.

Results may be summarized through tables, figures, or appropriate metrics. Raw test logs, exhaustive test cases, bug reports, and repetitive screenshots should generally be placed in the Appendix.

C. User Evaluation Results

Presents the findings from the user or stakeholder evaluation described in Chapter IV. Include, where applicable:

  • participant characteristics relevant to the evaluation;
  • task completion or evaluation outcomes;
  • usability findings;
  • acceptance findings;
  • effectiveness or satisfaction findings;
  • quantitative results;
  • qualitative feedback; and
  • recurring themes or observations.
Organize by purpose — Results should be organized according to the evaluation dimensions and study objectives rather than simply reproducing questionnaire responses.

D. Discussion of Findings

Interprets the findings and explains their significance. The discussion should address questions such as:

  1. What do the results indicate about the developed system?
  2. To what extent did the system meet the identified requirements?
  3. To what extent were the study objectives achieved?
  4. What strengths and limitations emerged from testing and evaluation?
  5. How do the findings relate to the theoretical or conceptual framework?
  6. How do the findings compare with relevant existing systems or prior studies?
  7. What insights can be drawn from the development and evaluation of the system?

Interpret, don't repeat

Students should not merely repeat results already presented in Chapters V-B and V-C. The discussion should explain what the findings mean and why they matter.

Report each objective — For each specific objective, state whether it was met, partially met, or not met against its criterion for success in Chapter IV-C, citing the evidence from Chapters V-B and V-C.

Where appropriate, the organization of Chapter V may follow the specific objectives established in Chapter I to maintain clear alignment across the manuscript.

08 · The Six Chapters

Chapter VI. Conclusion and Recommendations

This chapter synthesizes the overall contribution of the completed study and identifies appropriate directions arising from its findings.

A. Conclusion

Presents the conclusions that can reasonably be drawn from the study. The conclusion should:

  • synthesize the major findings;
  • indicate the extent to which the objectives were achieved, based on the assessment in Chapter V-D;
  • explain how the developed system addressed the identified problem;
  • highlight the overall contribution of the study; and
  • acknowledge important limitations where relevant.
Stay within the evidence. The conclusion should be based on the evidence presented in Chapter V. It should not introduce new findings or make claims that were not supported by the study.

B. Recommendations

Presents practical and future-oriented recommendations arising from the findings and limitations of the study. Recommendations may include:

  • system improvements;
  • additional features or integrations;
  • deployment or implementation recommendations;
  • organizational or operational recommendations;
  • further technical enhancements;
  • future evaluation directions; and
  • future research opportunities.
Remember — Recommendations should follow logically from the results and limitations of the study rather than serve as a general wish list of possible features.
09 · Back Matter

References

The References section is a required part of the manuscript. It is placed after the last chapter: Chapter IV for IS 295a, or Chapter VI for IS 295b.

It lists all sources cited in the manuscript, including academic studies, books, technical documentation, existing systems, frameworks, standards, and other works that informed the study.

Use APA format — Both the reference list and the in-text citations should follow APA style (7th edition).
ElementAPA requirement
In-text citationsCite sources in the body using the author–date format, e.g., (Santos, 2024) or Santos (2024). Include page numbers for direct quotations, e.g., (Santos, 2024, p. 15).
Reference listList entries alphabetically by the first author's surname, with a hanging indent, following APA formatting for each source type.
  • Every source cited in the text should appear in the References, and every entry in the References should be cited in the text.
  • Each source should be verified to exist and to support the claim for which it is cited.
Remember — Sources suggested or generated by AI tools must be checked against the actual publication before they are cited.

Declaration of AI Use

The Declaration of AI Use is a required part of the manuscript and is placed after the References and before the Appendices.

It discloses whether and how AI tools were used in conducting the study and preparing the manuscript. Where applicable, the declaration should state:

  • the AI tools used;
  • the purposes for which they were used, such as ideation, literature search, coding assistance, data analysis, writing, or editing;
  • the parts of the study or manuscript in which they were used; and
  • how AI-generated outputs were reviewed, verified, and revised.

12 · Putting It Together

General Guidance on Manuscript Coherence

The six chapters should not be treated as independent requirements. They should form a coherent progression.

Problem & Objectives→ Related Works & Gap→ Design & Development→ Testing & Evaluation Methodology→ System, Results & Discussion→ Conclusions & Recommendations

Students should maintain clear alignment throughout the manuscript:

ElementShould…
ProblemJustify the objectives
ObjectivesGuide the system design and methodology
Criteria for successDefine what counts as achieving each objective
RequirementsInform the system's features and design
MethodologyProduce evidence capable of addressing the objectives
Developed systemDemonstrate how the requirements were implemented
ResultsReport evidence from testing and evaluation
DiscussionInterpret what that evidence means
ConclusionsBe supported by the results

What each stage establishes

IS 295a · Chapters I–IV
  • A defensible and feasible plan for the study
IS 295b · Chapters I–VI
  • The completed study, carrying out the approved IS 295a plan, with Chapters I–IV updated in tense and any adviser-approved changes reflected
The final manuscript — should read as an academic account of a completed information systems study, not as a project proposal, software manual, technical documentation package, or repository of raw data and screenshots.
13 · Wrap-up

Key Takeaways

  1. IS 295a is a feasible plan, not a preliminary idea. Chapters I–IV should show the problem is investigated, the solution justified, and the methods achievable.
  2. The approved IS 295a proposal is your blueprint. IS 295b carries out that plan as closely as possible; Chapters I–IV are updated to past or present tense. Any substantive change should be justified and approved by your adviser.
  3. Chapter I moves from broad context to a specific problem, with objectives that align with the methodology, results, and conclusions.
  4. Significance explains value, not just names. State what each stakeholder specifically gains.
  5. Limitations are genuine boundaries, not justifications for avoidable shortcomings.
  6. Chapter II synthesizes; the gap emerges from the review. "No similar system exists" is not a gap analysis.
  7. Chapter III stays at an academic level of abstraction. Requirements are traceable to Chapter I; frameworks are meaningfully integrated; code-level artifacts go to the Appendix.
  8. Chapter IV fits testing and evaluation to the system and objectives. Use analytical methods only when they answer the study's questions, and link each objective to its evidence and criterion for success (Chapter IV-C).
  9. Chapter V is selective and interpretive. Purposeful, captioned screenshots; results organized by objectives; a discussion that explains what findings mean.
  10. Chapter VI stays within the evidence. Conclusions rest on Chapter V; recommendations follow from results and limitations, not a wish list.
  11. References and the Declaration of AI Use are required. Both come before the Appendices; cite only verified sources and disclose how AI tools were used.
