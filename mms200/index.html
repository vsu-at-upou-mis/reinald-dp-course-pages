From Research Blueprint to Completed Study

MMS 200 Manuscript Structure and Guidelines

A chapter-by-chapter guide to documenting a systematic research study in Multimedia Studies — from the research problem and its scholarly and theoretical foundations, through the research methodology, to findings, conclusions, and recommendations.

Prepared by: Dr. Reinald Adrian Pugoy — First Semester, AY 2026–2027
01 · Overview

Purpose of the Manuscript

The MMS 200 manuscript documents a systematic research study in the field of Multimedia Studies.

The manuscript should demonstrate the student's ability to:

  1. Identify and investigate a relevant research problem.
  2. Establish its theoretical and scholarly foundations.
  3. Employ an appropriate research methodology.
  4. Systematically collect and analyze evidence.
  5. Develop meaningful conclusions and contributions.

A Broad Range of Research

MMS 200 encompasses a broad range of research topics, approaches, and methodologies within Multimedia Studies. These may include qualitative, quantitative, mixed-methods, experimental, descriptive, exploratory, comparative, critical, design-oriented, and development-oriented research, among others.

Research topics may examine multimedia technologies, digital communication, media production and consumption, human-computer interaction, digital cultures, emerging technologies, user experiences, and the social, cultural, educational, and technological dimensions of multimedia.

Depending on the research problem and objectives, a study may involve empirical investigation, theoretical or conceptual analysis, evaluation of existing practices or technologies, design and development of multimedia systems or artifacts, or other forms of scholarly inquiry.

Whatever the approach, the standard is the same.

Regardless of the research approach, the manuscript should demonstrate academic rigor, methodological appropriateness, coherence, and a clear contribution to knowledge or practice.

MMS 200a: Research Proposal and Blueprint Stage

MMS 200a covers Chapters I to IV of the manuscript. At this stage, the manuscript establishes the research problem, reviews and synthesizes relevant literature, develops the theoretical and conceptual foundations of the study, and presents a detailed methodology for conducting the research.

MMS 200a serves as the approved research blueprint for implementation in MMS 200b. The proposal should therefore demonstrate that:

  • the research problem is clearly defined, significant, and appropriate for Multimedia Studies;
  • the research questions and objectives are sufficiently focused and achievable;
  • the study is grounded in relevant literature, theories, and concepts;
  • the research design and methodology are appropriate for addressing the research questions;
  • the data sources, participants, materials, or research contexts have been identified, where applicable;
  • the procedures for data collection, analysis, and interpretation are sufficiently developed;
  • ethical considerations and potential limitations have been addressed; and
  • the proposed study is feasible within the available time and resources.

Because MMS 200a represents the proposal stage, future-oriented language may be used when describing activities that have not yet been undertaken. However, the manuscript should already present a complete, coherent, and defensible research plan, rather than a preliminary research idea.

Approval of MMS 200a signifies that the student has established a sufficiently developed research blueprint for implementation in MMS 200b.

MMS 200b: Research Implementation and Completion Stage

MMS 200b covers the complete manuscript, Chapters I to VI, including the implementation of the approved research methodology, presentation and analysis of findings, discussion, conclusions, and recommendations.

MMS 200b is primarily concerned with implementing the research blueprint established in MMS 200a and completing the study. Students are expected to carry out the approved research activities, which may include data collection, experimentation, fieldwork, interviews, surveys, content analysis, artifact development, evaluation, or other procedures appropriate to their research design.

The MMS 200b manuscript should read as a completed research study rather than a proposal. Accordingly, Chapters I to IV should be reviewed and revised to reflect the research as actually conducted. Future-oriented statements should be replaced with descriptions of the actual research procedures, participants, data sources, instruments, analytical methods, and other relevant activities.

MMS 200a (Proposal)MMS 200b (Completed Study)
"The study will examine...""The study examined..."
"The researchers will conduct interviews...""Interviews were conducted..."
"The study will employ thematic analysis...""The data were analyzed using thematic analysis..."
"The proposed framework will guide...""The framework guided..."
"The researchers will evaluate...""The researchers evaluated..."

The final manuscript should also account for any justified modifications made during research implementation. It should not simply reproduce the MMS 200a proposal and append the findings. Instead, the entire document should present a coherent academic account of the research as actually conducted, the evidence obtained, and the conclusions drawn.

The approved MMS 200a proposal is your blueprint.

MMS 200b is not intended as an opportunity to begin an entirely new study or fundamentally redesign the approved research project.

Substantial changes to the research problem, objectives, scope, or methodology should be discussed with and approved by your adviser before implementation.

03 · The Six Chapters

Chapter I. Introduction

This chapter establishes the foundation of the study by presenting the research context, problem, objectives, significance, and boundaries.

A. Background of the Study

Provides the context and justification for undertaking the research. This section should explain:

  • the broader academic, technological, social, cultural, or practical context of the study;
  • the phenomenon, issue, problem, or area of inquiry being investigated;
  • relevant developments, trends, practices, or conditions surrounding the research topic;
  • what is currently known about the problem or phenomenon;
  • unresolved questions, limitations, tensions, or gaps that warrant investigation;
  • the relevance of the problem to Multimedia Studies; and
  • why conducting the research is important.

The discussion should progressively move from the broader context toward the specific research problem.

Broader context→ What is known→ Gaps & tensions→ Specific research problem
Build a rationale, not a fact sheet. Students should avoid presenting the background as a collection of loosely related facts or general descriptions of technologies. The background should establish a clear rationale for why the study is necessary.
Guiding question — What is the context of the research problem, and why does it warrant investigation?

B. Statement of the Problem

Clearly defines the central research problem or issue that the study seeks to investigate. This section should:

  • translate the broader background into a focused research problem;
  • identify the specific phenomenon, relationship, experience, practice, issue, or gap being investigated;
  • clarify what remains insufficiently understood, explained, evaluated, or addressed;
  • establish the significance of investigating the problem; and
  • formulate research questions appropriate to the nature of the study.

The statement of the problem may be presented as a general problem statement followed by specific research questions. Research questions may be exploratory, descriptive, explanatory, comparative, evaluative, or interpretive, depending on the research approach. They should be sufficiently focused to guide the research design and analysis.

Guiding question — What exactly does the study seek to investigate, understand, explain, or evaluate?

C. Objectives of the Study

States what the research aims to accomplish.

Two levels of objectives

General Objective
  • States the overall purpose of the research and directly corresponds to the central research problem.
Specific Objectives
  • Break down the general objective into concrete, focused, and assessable objectives.

Depending on the nature of the study, objectives may involve:

  • identifying or describing patterns, characteristics, or practices;
  • examining experiences, perceptions, attitudes, or behaviors;
  • analyzing relationships among variables or constructs;
  • exploring meanings, interpretations, or social processes;
  • comparing groups, contexts, platforms, or approaches;
  • evaluating the effectiveness, usability, accessibility, or implications of multimedia technologies or interventions;
  • developing and evaluating a conceptual framework, model, or multimedia artifact; or
  • generating insights or recommendations based on empirical findings.

The objectives should align with the research questions, methodology, analysis, and conclusions.

Objectives are not procedures. Students should distinguish between research objectives and procedural activities. Activities such as conducting interviews, distributing questionnaires, or reviewing literature are generally methodological steps rather than research objectives in themselves.
Guiding question — What does the study intend to accomplish through the investigation?

D. Significance of the Study

Explains the academic, practical, technological, social, cultural, or methodological value of the research. Depending on the study, its contributions may include:

  • advancing understanding of a multimedia-related phenomenon;
  • contributing empirical evidence to an existing body of knowledge;
  • extending, examining, or refining relevant theories or conceptual frameworks;
  • informing multimedia design, development, communication, or production practices;
  • providing insights for educators, practitioners, policymakers, organizations, or communities;
  • identifying implications for users, audiences, or other stakeholders; and
  • establishing directions for future research.
Remember — Explain the specific contribution of the study rather than merely listing potential beneficiaries. Claims of significance should be realistic and proportionate to the scope of the research.
Guiding question — Why is this research important, and what meaningful contribution can it make?

E. Scope and Limitations

Defines the boundaries and constraints of the research. This section should explain:

  • what the study covers, including its focus, participants or data sources, and research context;
  • what falls outside the scope of the investigation; and
  • relevant methodological, practical, or contextual limitations that may affect the findings.
ScopeLimitations
What the study deliberately includes or excludesConstraints that may affect the research or the interpretation of findings
Guiding question — What does the study cover, and what are its boundaries and limitations?

Chapter II. Review of Related Literature

This chapter situates the research within existing scholarly knowledge, relevant theories, empirical studies, and established approaches.

It should demonstrate the student's understanding of the research domain, identify relevant gaps, and establish how the present study contributes to ongoing scholarly discussions.

Analyze, critique, and synthesize — don't just summarize. The chapter should be analytical, critical, and synthetic rather than merely descriptive. Students should avoid simply summarizing one article or study after another.

A. Scholarly Foundations and Previous Research

Presents a critical and synthesized discussion of existing scholarly literature, theoretical perspectives, empirical studies, and other relevant works related to the research problem. Depending on the study, this may include:

  • foundational concepts and definitions;
  • relevant theories, models, and conceptual perspectives;
  • previous empirical studies and their findings;
  • established methodologies and analytical approaches;
  • relevant technological, social, cultural, or institutional developments;
  • existing multimedia technologies, platforms, practices, or interventions;
  • scholarly debates and competing interpretations; and
  • strengths and limitations of previous research.

The review should be organized thematically or conceptually, integrating relevant theories, concepts, methodologies, and empirical findings. Students should examine:

Relationships· Agreements· Contradictions· Patterns· Limitations

across existing works rather than merely summarizing individual publications. The discussion should establish how existing scholarship informs the present research.

B. Synthesis, Research Gap, and Positioning of the Study

Integrates the reviewed literature and studies to establish the scholarly basis for the research, and explains how the present study is positioned within the existing body of knowledge. This section should explain:

  1. What existing literature and research collectively establish.
  2. Areas of agreement or disagreement.
  3. Important patterns and emerging insights.
  4. Limitations of previous studies.
  5. Unresolved theoretical, empirical, contextual, or methodological issues.
  6. The specific gap or opportunity addressed by the present study.
Remember — The research gap should emerge logically from the preceding review. A gap does not necessarily require demonstrating that no previous study exists on the topic. It may involve an underexplored context, insufficient evidence, unresolved contradiction, methodological limitation, or need for further theoretical understanding.

Having established the gap, this section should then clarify:

  • how the study responds to the identified research gap;
  • how it builds upon, extends, challenges, or complements previous research;
  • the distinctive perspective, context, methodology, or evidence it introduces; and
  • the contribution it intends to make to Multimedia Studies.
What is established→ What remains unresolved→ The research gap→ How this study responds

The discussion should establish the study's scholarly justification without overstating its originality or significance.

Anticipated vs. demonstrated contribution — This section presents the anticipated contribution of the study. In MMS 200b, update its tense only; the contribution actually demonstrated by the completed study is discussed in Chapter V.

Chapter III. Theoretical and Conceptual Framework

This chapter establishes the theoretical and conceptual foundations guiding the research.

It explains the perspectives, concepts, constructs, assumptions, or relationships that inform how the research problem is understood and investigated. The framework should be appropriate to the research questions and methodology.

Writing guidance. Frameworks should be meaningfully integrated into the study rather than included merely to satisfy a manuscript requirement. The level of theoretical elaboration should be proportionate to the nature and objectives of the research.

A. Theoretical Framework

Presents the theory or theories that inform the research. Depending on the study, this section may include:

  • relevant established theories;
  • key theoretical concepts and assumptions;
  • important constructs or dimensions;
  • relationships or propositions suggested by the theory;
  • rationale for selecting the theoretical perspective; and
  • explanation of how the theory informs the research questions, methodology, or interpretation.
Apply, don't just describe — Explain the applicability of the selected theory to the specific research problem. Merely describing a theory without demonstrating its relevance to the study is insufficient.

For research approaches that do not begin with a fixed theoretical framework, students should explain the guiding scholarly perspective or justify the approach adopted.

B. Conceptual Framework and Its Application to the Study

Presents the key concepts, constructs, dimensions, or relationships underlying the investigation and explains how they guide the research. Depending on the research approach, this section should:

  • identify and explain the central concepts or constructs;
  • describe their relationships or conceptual organization;
  • present a conceptual diagram, where appropriate;
  • explain how the framework informs the research questions and objectives;
  • establish its connection to the research design, instruments, or analytical approach; and
  • clarify how the framework guides the interpretation of findings.
Concepts & constructs→ Research questions→ Design & instruments→ Analysis→ Interpretation

The conceptual framework should be grounded in relevant literature and demonstrate a clear connection to the research problem. Where applicable, important concepts or constructs should be sufficiently defined to clarify how they are understood, examined, or measured in the study.

Explain every diagram — A conceptual diagram may be provided where appropriate, but it should be accompanied by an explanation of its components and relationships.
Note — Depending on the research approach, the theoretical and conceptual frameworks may be integrated or adapted into an appropriate analytical framework. Discuss the appropriate structure with your adviser.

Chapter IV. Research Methodology

This chapter explains how the research is designed and conducted to address the research questions and achieve the study objectives.

For MMS 200a, this chapter presents the planned research methodology and serves as the methodological blueprint for implementation. For MMS 200b, it should document the methodology and procedures actually implemented, including any justified modifications to the approved research plan.

Writing guidance. The methodology should provide sufficient detail for readers to understand how the research was conducted and assess its methodological rigor. The specific content may vary depending on the research approach.

A. Research Design and Approach

Describes the overall research approach and specific research design adopted in the study. This section should explain:

  • the research approach, whether qualitative, quantitative, mixed-methods, or another appropriate approach;
  • the specific research design or strategy;
  • the rationale for selecting the approach and design; and
  • how the design addresses the research questions and objectives.

Depending on the research problem, possible research designs may include:

Descriptive· Exploratory· Correlational· Experimental· Case study· Phenomenological· Ethnographic· Comparative· Action research· Design-based· Developmental
Justify, don't define — Explain why the selected research design is appropriate, rather than merely providing its textbook definition.

B. Research Setting, Participants, and Data Sources

Describes the research context and identifies the participants, materials, or sources of evidence involved. Depending on the study, this section may include:

  • the research setting or environment;
  • the target population or participants;
  • sampling strategy and sample size;
  • participant selection or recruitment criteria;
  • datasets, documents, multimedia materials, or other sources of evidence; and
  • units of analysis.

The selection of participants or data sources should be appropriate to the research questions and methodology. Students should provide sufficient justification for their sampling or selection decisions.

Note — Not all studies involve human participants. This section should be adapted to the nature of the research.

C. Research Instruments and Materials

Describes the instruments, tools, materials, or resources used in conducting the study. Depending on the research approach, these may include:

  • questionnaires or survey instruments;
  • interview or focus group discussion guides;
  • observation protocols;
  • experimental materials or stimuli;
  • coding schemes or analytical frameworks;
  • multimedia artifacts or digital platforms;
  • software applications or computational tools; and
  • other research-specific instruments or materials.

Students should explain the purpose, development, selection, and appropriateness of the instruments or materials. Where applicable, procedures for establishing validity, reliability, or other relevant quality criteria should also be discussed.

Detailed instruments and supporting documentation may be placed in the Appendices.

D. Research Procedures

Explains the systematic procedures followed in conducting the research. Depending on the study, this section may describe:

  • preliminary preparations and coordination;
  • participant recruitment and informed consent;
  • instrument development and validation;
  • data collection procedures;
  • interviews, surveys, observations, or fieldwork;
  • experiments or interventions;
  • collection or extraction of digital content;
  • design, development, or implementation activities;
  • evaluation procedures; and
  • other relevant research activities.

For every procedure, explain three things

What
  • What was done
How
  • How it was done
Why
  • Why the procedures were appropriate

The procedures should be presented in a logical sequence. Relevant ethical considerations, including informed consent, privacy, confidentiality, data protection, and necessary research approvals, should be discussed within the applicable procedures. For studies involving multiple phases, a research procedure diagram or table may be included.

E. Data Analysis and Interpretation

Explains how the collected or generated evidence is processed, analyzed, and interpreted to address the research questions. Depending on the research approach, analytical methods may include:

  • descriptive or inferential statistics;
  • thematic analysis;
  • content or discourse analysis;
  • comparative analysis;
  • computational or multimedia analysis;
  • experimental data analysis;
  • evaluation of research outputs or artifacts;
  • integration of quantitative and qualitative findings; and
  • other appropriate analytical techniques.

This section should explain:

  1. How the data are prepared or processed.
  2. The analytical techniques employed.
  3. How these techniques address the research questions.
  4. How findings are interpreted.
  5. Relevant measures for ensuring the credibility, trustworthiness, or rigor of the analysis.
Purpose over complexity — Select analytical methods appropriate to the research objectives and data characteristics. Analytical complexity should be justified by the research problem. Advanced statistical or computational techniques should not be employed merely to make the study appear more sophisticated.
Quality and ethics run throughout. Research quality and ethical considerations should be integrated into the relevant methodological subsections rather than treated as isolated requirements. Demonstrate appropriate attention to validity, reliability, trustworthiness, transparency, and research ethics throughout the research process, as applicable to your chosen methodology.

Chapter V. Results and Discussion

This chapter presents the findings of the completed research and interprets their meaning in relation to the research questions, objectives, theoretical or conceptual framework, and relevant literature.

Chapter V forms part of MMS 200b and is not required as a completed-results chapter during the MMS 200a proposal stage. The chapter should provide a systematic account of the evidence generated through the research and demonstrate how the findings contribute to answering the research questions.

Writing guidance. Present organized and synthesized findings rather than raw data dumps. The emphasis should be on meaningful analysis and interpretation, supported by appropriate evidence. Avoid excessive repetition of tables, figures, screenshots, interview transcripts, statistical outputs, or other materials better suited for the Appendices.

A. Presentation of Research Findings

Presents the results obtained from the research. Depending on the study, findings may include:

  • descriptive characteristics of participants, datasets, or research materials;
  • statistical results;
  • identified patterns, trends, or relationships;
  • qualitative themes and categories;
  • participant experiences or perspectives;
  • findings from content, discourse, or multimedia analysis;
  • experimental or comparative results;
  • evaluation findings;
  • developed models, frameworks, or artifacts;
  • outcomes of design or development activities; and
  • other relevant research outputs.

The findings should be organized according to the research questions, specific objectives, themes, analytical dimensions, or another logical structure appropriate to the study.

Tables, figures, graphs, diagrams, excerpts, and other visual representations should be used where they improve clarity. Each table or figure should be accompanied by sufficient explanation.

Explain relevance — Avoid simply presenting numerical results, quotations, or visual materials without explaining their relevance.

B. Analysis and Discussion of Findings

Analyzes and interprets the findings in relation to the research questions, objectives, relevant literature, and theoretical or conceptual frameworks. This section should:

  1. Explain the meaning and significance of the findings.
  2. Identify important patterns, relationships, themes, or contradictions.
  3. Examine how the findings support, extend, challenge, or differ from previous studies.
  4. Interpret the findings using relevant theoretical or conceptual perspectives.
  5. Discuss unexpected or emerging findings.
  6. Consider relevant contextual factors.
  7. Explain the implications of the results.
  8. Explain the extent to which the research questions were answered and the objectives achieved.
  9. Identify the theoretical, empirical, methodological, or practical contributions demonstrated by the study.
  10. Acknowledge important qualifications or limitations affecting the interpretation of findings.

Reporting vs. interpreting

Reporting
  • Describes what the evidence shows.
Interpretation
  • Explains the meaning, significance, or implications of that evidence.

The discussion should engage critically with relevant scholarship rather than merely repeat the literature review. Claims should be supported by the actual findings and should remain within the boundaries of the research design.

The discussion should also move beyond individual findings to explain what the study establishes as a whole, setting up the conclusions in Chapter VI.

Note — The organization of Chapter V may vary depending on the research approach. Sections A and B may be integrated where appropriate, particularly for qualitative studies in which presenting findings and interpreting them together produces a more coherent discussion. Findings and discussion may be organized thematically or according to the research questions, subject to adviser approval.

Chapter VI. Conclusion and Recommendations

This chapter synthesizes the overall contribution of the completed research and identifies appropriate implications and directions arising from its findings.

It should provide a clear and evidence-based conclusion to the study without introducing new data or unsupported claims.

A. Conclusion

Presents the conclusions that can reasonably be drawn from the research. The conclusion should:

  • synthesize the major findings;
  • directly address the central research problem;
  • indicate the extent to which the research questions were answered;
  • explain how the objectives were achieved;
  • highlight the principal insights and contributions of the study;
  • identify relevant theoretical, practical, or methodological implications; and
  • acknowledge important limitations where appropriate.

The conclusion should not simply repeat the results presented in Chapter V. Instead, it should articulate what the research ultimately establishes and why those findings matter.

Stay within the evidence. Conclusions should be proportionate to the evidence and scope of the study. Avoid making broad claims that exceed what your research design and findings can support.

B. Recommendations

Presents recommendations arising from the findings, conclusions, and limitations of the study. Depending on the research, recommendations may include:

  • directions for future research;
  • further investigation of emerging questions;
  • methodological improvements or alternative research approaches;
  • practical applications of the findings;
  • recommendations for multimedia design, development, or production;
  • improvements to existing practices, technologies, or interventions;
  • organizational, educational, or policy recommendations;
  • further development or evaluation of research outputs; and
  • opportunities for extending the study to other populations, settings, or contexts.
Remember — Recommendations should follow logically from the research findings and identified limitations. They should be sufficiently specific and realistic, not a generic wish list of possible activities that are not supported by the study.
09 · Back Matter

References

The References section is a required part of the manuscript. It is placed after the last chapter: Chapter IV for MMS 200a, or Chapter VI for MMS 200b.

It lists all sources cited in the manuscript, including academic studies, books, technical documentation, existing systems, frameworks, standards, and other works that informed the study.

Use APA format — Both the reference list and the in-text citations should follow APA style (7th edition).
ElementAPA requirement
In-text citationsCite sources in the body using the author–date format, e.g., (Santos, 2024) or Santos (2024). Include page numbers for direct quotations, e.g., (Santos, 2024, p. 15).
Reference listList entries alphabetically by the first author's surname, with a hanging indent, following APA formatting for each source type.
  • Every source cited in the text should appear in the References, and every entry in the References should be cited in the text.
  • Each source should be verified to exist and to support the claim for which it is cited.
Remember — Sources suggested or generated by AI tools must be checked against the actual publication before they are cited.

10 · Back Matter

Declaration of AI Use

This section provides a transparent account of the use of artificial intelligence (AI) tools in preparing and conducting the research.

Students should disclose:

  • the AI tools used, including their names and purposes;
  • how the tools were used in the research process, such as literature exploration, writing assistance, coding, data analysis, or multimedia production;
  • the extent of AI assistance in producing research outputs; and
  • how AI-generated content or outputs were reviewed, verified, and validated, where applicable.
Disclose even limited use. AI use should be disclosed even when limited to writing or language assistance. The declaration should distinguish between AI-assisted work and the student's own intellectual contributions. If no AI tools were used, students should explicitly state this.

Suggested Declaration Format

Declaration of AI Use — The researcher(s) declare(s) the use of the following artificial intelligence tools in the conduct and preparation of this study:
AI ToolPurpose of UseExtent of Use
[Tool name][e.g., language editing, coding assistance, data analysis][Brief description of how the tool was used]
[Tool name][Purpose][Extent of use]

All AI-assisted outputs were reviewed and verified as appropriate. The researcher(s) assume(s) full responsibility for the accuracy, originality, integrity, and final content of this manuscript.

For studies without AI use:

The researcher(s) declare(s) that no generative artificial intelligence tools were used in the conduct of the research or preparation of this manuscript.

AI assists. Researchers remain responsible.

Students remain fully responsible for the accuracy, originality, integrity, and ethical conduct of their research, regardless of whether AI tools were used.

MMS 200a
  • Covers AI use during proposal preparation.
MMS 200b
  • Updated to reflect AI use throughout the completed research, including implementation, analysis, and manuscript preparation.
11 · Back Matter

Appendices

The Appendices contain supplementary materials that support the research but are too detailed or extensive for inclusion in the main manuscript.

Depending on the study, these may include:

Possible Appendix Materials
  • Research instruments and questionnaires
  • Interview and focus group discussion guides
  • Informed consent forms
  • Ethics approval documents
  • Detailed coding schemes or codebooks
  • Supplementary tables and statistical outputs
  • Sample transcripts or anonymized excerpts
  • Additional figures or visual materials
  • Research protocols
  • Technical specifications
  • System or multimedia artifact screenshots
  • Supplementary design documents
  • Evaluation instruments
  • Relevant documentation of research procedures
  • Other supporting materials

Appendices should be included only when they provide meaningful supplementary evidence or documentation. Students should avoid turning the Appendices into an unorganized collection of raw materials.

The main manuscript must stand on its own. It should remain understandable without requiring readers to consult the Appendices for essential explanations, findings, or interpretations.

Developed systems and artifacts

Chapter V
  • Representative screenshots and visual demonstrations of major features or outputs, discussed as part of the research results.
Appendices
  • More extensive screenshots, interface inventories, detailed technical documentation, and supplementary implementation materials.
12 · Putting It Together

General Guidance on Manuscript Coherence

The six chapters should not be treated as independent requirements. They should form a coherent progression.

Problem & Objectives→ Literature & Research Gap→ Theoretical & Conceptual Foundations→ Research Methodology→ Results & Discussion→ Conclusions & Recommendations

Students should maintain clear alignment throughout the manuscript:

ElementShould…
Research problemEstablish the need for investigation
Questions & objectivesDefine what the study seeks to accomplish
Literature reviewEstablish the scholarly foundation and research gap
FrameworkGuide the investigation and interpretation, as appropriate
MethodologyProvide a systematic means of generating and analyzing evidence
ResultsDirectly address the research questions and objectives
DiscussionInterpret the findings in relation to relevant literature and frameworks
ConclusionsBe supported by the findings

Guidance for MMS 200a

For MMS 200a, this progression establishes a defensible and feasible research blueprint through Chapters I to IV. The manuscript should demonstrate that the student has moved beyond identifying an interesting topic and has developed a research plan that is sufficiently grounded, focused, and methodologically sound for implementation.

At the end of MMS 200a, the student should be prepared to proceed with the approved research activities without requiring a fundamental redesign of the study.

Guidance for MMS 200b

For MMS 200b, the manuscript should document the completed research through Chapters I to VI, with Chapters I to IV revised as necessary to accurately reflect the research as actually conducted.

The approved MMS 200a manuscript serves as the foundation for MMS 200b. Students should maintain continuity between the approved research blueprint and its implementation. Any substantial methodological changes should be justified and discussed with the adviser.

What each stage establishes

MMS 200a · Chapters I–IV
  • A defensible and feasible research blueprint, ready for implementation
MMS 200b · Chapters I–VI
  • A coherent account of the research process, findings, interpretation, and contributions
The final manuscript — should read as a completed academic research study, not as a research proposal, project progress report, technical manual, or repository of raw data and supplementary materials.
